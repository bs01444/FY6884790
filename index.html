Abstract

 

The internal combustion engine (ICE) has undergone monumental advancements throughout its existence, leading to vast improvements in its efficiency, performance, and emissions.

Central to these developments is the evolution of modern valve control systems, such as pneumatic valves as seen in F1, variable cam systems like VTEC, proprietary rotary valves being developed by Vaztec, and Koenigsegg’s cutting-edge Freevalve system.

These technologies have, and are, redefining how air and fuel interact in the combustion chamber, maximising volumetric efficiency and performance.

The following sections will outline the function, benefits, drawbacks, and applications of these solutions.

 

 

1. Fundamentals of Valve Control in Internal Combustion Engines

 

An Internal Combustion (4-Stroke Piston) Engine (ICE) is the most common powertrain in land vehicles. They require inlet and exhaust valves to control the flow of air into and out of the combustion chamber.

Valvetrain advancements in modern ICEs have led to modern and innovative solutions to this problem. Being able to independently control a valve’s opening timing, duration and extension means that the mass flowrate of air molecules entering/exiting the combustion chamber can be precisely controlled. This allows for better fuel mixing and stoichiometry (ratio of air and fuel needed for complete combustion), meaning significantly improved volumetric efficiency (a measure of an engines combustion efficiency), resulting in a more efficient, reliable, performing, and economical engine and vehicle.

This section is an overview of traditional versus current and future technologies that have been developed to reach these goals.

 

  • Traditional Camshaft-based Valves:

As mentioned previously, a traditional ICE uses poppet valves, typically either 1 or 2 for each intake and exhaust port in a cylinder. The opening and closing of these valves are timed to be 180° out of phase with each other, and complete 2 revolutions per crank revolution. This offers a great seal, preventing exhaust gasses from combustion escaping via the inlet valve, but their shape affects air flow, reducing the capability of a stoichiometric air-fuel mixture.

Since there is only one set of cams, there is no way to control the valve rate without changing RPM.

This can lead to stoichiometry issues at varying RPMs, commonly due to valve float at high values, and parasitic losses at lower ranges.

 

  • F1 Pneumatic Valves:

Formula 1 is at the forefront of technological advancements in ICE technology. One of the main breakthroughs in the sport was the invention and successful implementation of pneumatic valves.

The only conceptual difference between these and a traditional setup is that the valve springs are replaced by an incompressible gas. This offers the advantage of essentially eliminating valve float at high RPM, whilst maintaining a variable spring rate and improved durability.

  • Honda’s VTEC:

VTEC stands for Valve Timing Electronically Controlled. It describes the use of multiple sets of cam lobes, rather than a single set. It allows an engine’s cam profile to be set at two different rates, which can be mechanically switched to at a given RPM. This offers better efficiency and performance for both low and high rev-ranges.

 

  • Vaztec Rotary Valves:

https://vaztec.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Screen-Shot-2022-09-27-at-10.43.53-AM.png

A rotary valve is a rotating cylinder that allows air to pass through at different rates, depending on its opening angle. Picture the valve on a Bunsen burner; it operates in the same way but can be electronically controlled by a motor.

Rotary Vales have existed for a long time, but their use in ICEs has not yet been seen. Vaztec is a company at the forefront of their development and implementation for ICEs

 

  • Koenigsegg’s Freevalve:

Freevalve is a brand name used for electro-hydraulic-pneumatic valves. The utilisation of this valve technology trifecta provides an elegant solution.

Having this camless technology removes the need for a traditional camshaft, saving weight, and reducing losses.

The infinitely variable VVT, VVD and VVL means that theoretically perfect volumetric efficiency can be achieved throughout the rev-range of an engine. The sustainability and economical benefits to this are unprecedented in ICEs, meaning this technology is a great way to prolong the lifespan of ICE vehicles.

 

 

2. The Issue with Traditional Systems

 

Traditional camshaft-based systems are limited by mechanical constraints, reducing efficiency, emissions and performance.

These include valve float, and high inertia, leading to parasitic losses.

Valve float is when there is insufficient pressure to seat/close a valve. Valve float typically occurs at high RPMs, as the valve spring does not provide enough force to accelerate the valve shut. This leads to air leakage, meaning poor stoichiometry and volumetric efficiency.

This can be reduced by using stiffer springs, but then more force is required from combustion to open the valves, meaning less power is directed to the wheels of the vehicle, reducing performance. This is known as parasitic loss.

Another disadvantage of a camshaft-based system is that there is high rotational inertia from the heavy and wide camshaft. The force needed to rotate this also results in parasitic losses.

4. Applications and Future Outlook

 

  • Current Use Cases:

Technologies like VTEC, VANOS and VarioCam have existed for many years, and have therefore been commonly adopted in passenger vehicles. The efficiency and performance enhancements of these systems are pivotal to the success of modern ICEs.

Specific cases such as F1 Pneumatic Valves, and recently, the use of Freevalve in the Koenigsegg Gemera, have pushed the limits of research, creativity, and manufacture of performance focused applications.

 

  • Future Trends:

The future of ICE valve technology lies heavily in the use of these solutions. A key factor, pushing their development is a strive to reduce emission in an endeavour to stop climate change. These systems will achieve that, whilst also improving overall vehicle efficiency and performance.

As we progress, we can anticipate even more innovation, leading to improvements in all relevant engineering industries.

 

 

5. Conclusion

Valve control technologies and their development are essential to the progression of efficient, sustainable, durable and high-performance vehicles. While there have already been massive advancements in this industry, there are inevitable improvements that can be made to bridge the gap between the cars of today with the vehicles of the future.

The systems discussed offer unique and innovative solutions, not just for the automotive industry, but all mechanical engineering sectors.

By embracing these technologies, automakers can achieve better performance and reduced negative environmental impact, ensuring the ICE remains relevant in a rapidly evolving automotive landscape.
