Variable Valve Control in Internal Combustion Engines: VVT, VVD, and Freevalve Technology

The internal combustion engine (ICE) has undergone monumental advancements throughout its existence, leading to vast improvements in its efficiency, performance, and emissions.

Central to these developments is the evolution of valve control systems, such as pneumatic valves as seen in F1, variable cam systems like VTEC, proprietary rotary valves being developed by Vaztec, and Koenigsegg’s cutting-edge Freevalve system.

These technologies have, and are, redefining how air and fuel interact in the combustion chamber, maximising volumetric efficiency and performance.

I am going to be discussing the function, benefits, drawbacks, and applications of these solutions.

1. Fundamentals of Valve Control in Internal Combustion Engines

An Internal Combustion (4-Stroke Piston) Engine (ICE) is the most common powertrain in land vehicles. They require inlet and exhaust valves to control the flow of air into and out of the combustion chamber.

Valvetrain advancements in modern ICEs have led to modern and innovative solutions to this problem. This section is an overview of traditional versus current and future technologies.

 

  • Traditional Camshaft-based Valves:

As mentioned previously, a traditional ICE uses poppet valves, typically either 1 or 2 for each intake and exhaust port in a cylinder. The opening and closing of these valves are timed to be 180° out of phase with each other, and complete 2 revolutions per crank revolution.

Since there is only one set of cams, there is no way to control the valve rate without changing RPM.

This can lead to stoichiometry issues at varying RPM’s, commonly due to valve float at high values, and parasitic losses at lower ranges.

 

  • F1 Pneumatic Valves:

Formula 1 is at the forefront of technological advancements in ICE technology. One of the main breakthroughs in the sport was the invention and successful implementation of pneumatic valves.

The only conceptual difference between these and a traditional setup is that the valve springs are replaced by an incompressible gas. This offers the advantage of essentially eliminating valve float at high RPM, whilst maintaining a variable spring rate and improved durability.

2. The issue with traditional systems

Traditional camshaft-based systems are limited by mechanical constraints, reducing efficiency, emissions and performance.

These include valve float, and high inertia, leading to parasitic losses.

Valve float is when there is insufficient pressure to seat/close a valve. Valve float typically occurs at high RPMs, as the valve spring does not provide enough force to accelerate the valve shut. This leads to air leakage, meaning poor stoichiometry and volumetric efficiency.

This can be reduced by using stiffer springs, but then more force is required from combustion to open the valves, meaning less power is directed to the wheels of the vehicle, reducing performance. This is known as parasitic loss.

Another disadvantage of a camshaft-based system is that there is high rotational inertia from the heavy and wide camshaft. The force needed to rotate this also results in parasitic losses.

 

 

 

3. Freevalve Technology: A Revolution

Freevalve, developed by Koenigsegg, represents a leap forward by eliminating the camshaft entirely. Instead, each valve operates independently using actuators controlled by an electronic system.

Key Features:

  1. Independent Valve Operation:
    • Intake and exhaust valves function autonomously, enabling custom timing and duration for each cylinder.
  2. Pneumatic-Hydraulic-Electric Actuators (PHEA):
    • Actuators replace mechanical components, reducing weight and friction.
  3. Real-Time Adjustability:
    • On-the-fly adjustments optimize performance for specific driving conditions.

Advantages:

  • Increased Efficiency: Precise control over air-fuel dynamics boosts thermal efficiency.
  • Enhanced Performance: Achieves higher torque and power output at lower RPMs.
  • Lower Emissions: Enables advanced combustion strategies, including Miller and Atkinson cycles.
  • Flexibility: Adaptable for various fuels, including hydrogen and synthetic options.

5. Applications and Future Outlook

  • Current Use Cases:
    • VVT and VVD are standard in modern engines for passenger cars.
    • Freevalve is found in high-performance vehicles like Koenigsegg’s hypercars.
  • Future Trends:
    • Integration with hybrid systems.
    • Applications in hydrogen ICEs for zero-carbon solutions.
    • Advancements in actuator reliability and cost-effectiveness.

6. Conclusion

Valve control technologies such as VVT, VVD, and Freevalve illustrate the ongoing innovation in ICEs. While VVT and VVD remain practical for widespread use, Freevalve’s camless architecture offers unmatched flexibility and efficiency, paving the way for the next generation of engines.

By embracing these technologies, automakers can achieve better performance and reduced environmental impact, ensuring the ICE remains relevant in a rapidly evolving automotive landscape.
